Look at the bottom of your passport's photo page, or the back of a national ID card, and you will see two or three lines of capital letters, numbers and `<` characters that look like a typo. That is the **MRZ — the machine-readable zone** — and it is the part of the document every one of our guides tells you to cover before sharing a copy. Here is what it actually is.

## What the MRZ is

The MRZ is a standardised strip of text designed to be read by a machine, not a person. It follows **[ICAO Document 9303](https://www.icao.int/publications/doc-series/doc-9303)**, the same international standard used for passports and many national ID cards worldwide, which is why a border scanner in one country can read a passport issued by another. Two lines on a passport (44 characters each), or three shorter lines on many ID cards — either way, the format is fixed and public, which means anyone with a basic reader — or a phone camera and free software — can decode it instantly.

## What information it encodes

Reading left to right, the MRZ packs in:

- **Document type and issuing country.**
- **Your full name**, exactly as printed on the document.
- **The document number.**
- **Your nationality.**
- **Date of birth.**
- **Sex.**
- **Document expiry date.**
- **Check digits** — extra characters a machine uses to confirm the other fields haven't been tampered with.

In other words: almost everything a system needs to identify you, in a format built to be copied by software rather than typed by hand.

## Why it matters more than the rest of the page

A printed name and photo tell a *person* who you are. The MRZ tells a *machine* — instantly, and at scale. That is exactly why it is the field most valuable to identity fraud: it is what automated systems for opening accounts, verifying age or registering a SIM card are built to read, and a clean photo of it copies with perfect accuracy. Hiding your document number and date of birth in the printed text but leaving the MRZ untouched defeats the point, since the MRZ repeats both — and more — in a form that's even easier to lift.

## How to hide it properly

A solid, opaque box over both (or all three) full lines is enough — partial redaction that leaves part of a line readable can still expose enough for the check-digit structure to be reconstructed.
